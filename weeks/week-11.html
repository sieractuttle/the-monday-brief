The Plan Is Fine. Can the Office Run It?

Why good strategies stall inside the organizations built to run them, and what that means for a one-person recruiting office changing systems mid-year.

How organizations carry out a strategy

A strategy is a set of choices. An organization is the thing that has to make those choices happen every day. Chandler's old argument is that structure should follow strategy, and the trouble starts when it doesn't. A structure built for the last strategy keeps rewarding the last strategy's behavior long after a new one is announced. Galbraith's Star Model gives the checklist: strategy, structure, processes, rewards, and people all have to point the same way. The point most often left behind is rewards, because people redesign the chart and the workflow and leave the old incentives running underneath.

Structure is also a way of handling coordination. Thompson separates tasks that only share a resource, tasks that pass work down a line, and tasks that need constant back and forth. The type of dependency should decide how much coordination you build, and most coordination pain comes from a back and forth task being run like a hand-off line. Lawrence and Lorsch add the tension underneath: you divide work so people can get good at it, and then you have to put it back together.

Fit is the test. Nadler and Tushman say strategy, work, people, and the formal and informal organization all have to match, and a mismatch shows up quietly as a performance gap nobody can explain. Porter's version is that activities which reinforce each other are harder to copy and harder to break by accident.

Then comes the problem of running two clocks. March's point is that using what you already know and searching for what is next compete for the same scarce time, money, and attention. Firms that only use what they have get efficient and fragile. Firms that only search get creative and broke. Large firms often split the two into separate units joined at the top. Gibson and Birkinshaw show smaller teams can manage it inside individual people.

The second half is change. Lewin's unfreeze, change, refreeze and Kotter's eight errors give the process view. Beer and Nohria add that change driven only by economics or only by capability usually fails, and that the efforts that last do both in sequence. Weick adds what the process view misses: people don't experience a change as a plan. They experience it as a disruption to the story they tell about their own work. That is why Petriglieri's identity threat matters. People resist changes that threaten what they are good at, even helpful ones, and the resistance carries information. Piderit adds that the common response is not for or against. It is wanting and not wanting at once.

Technology sharpens this. Dietvorst and colleagues find people drop a statistical model faster than they drop a human who made the same mistake, and that letting people adjust the output even slightly recovers trust. Kellogg and colleagues give a vocabulary for what a system does to work: it can replace, restrict, recommend, record, rate, or reward. Naming which of those a tool is doing is the first step to seeing why someone objects to it.

The ideas this chapter runs on

Applied to the RN-BS program

1. Structure follows strategy (Chandler, Galbraith, Nadler and Tushman, Porter)

Week 10 ended with a strategy: home, near, and far tiers, with $166 per lead as the bar a trip outside Idaho has to beat. This week asks whether the office can run it. Taking the Star Model point by point:

Strategy: tiered and regional, with a bar for trips. Structure: one person covering what used to be two jobs. Processes: events and lead follow-up moving from Marketing Cloud to Slate. The October 21 Free Application Day went out as a Marketing Cloud journey, and info sessions and free app days from here on run through Slate. People: the skill to run Slate, and time.

Rewards is the weak point. If success is counted in leads, a $5,814.08 trip that produced 35 leads still looks like progress, and the $166 bar quietly loses to the old measure. Christin's work on metrics at work is the warning. People tune what they do to satisfy the number, even when it drifts from the goal.

The coordination point shows up at the handoff from event to follow-up to application, which is the Week 7 handoff rule again. Event to follow-up is a line. A CC partner and the program deciding together which events happen is a two-way dependency, and that second one is where I'd expect the friction.

A structure holds when all five points pull the same way. One point still pulling the old way is enough to bend the rest.

2. Two clocks (March, Tushman and O'Reilly, Gibson and Birkinshaw)

The office runs two clocks. The first is working what already works: Idaho CC events, partner relationships with places like CWI and Lewis-Clark State, info sessions, and the Free Application Days. The second is testing what might: the purchased-list test, near-tier ads, and Slate itself. March's warning is that these fight for the same hours. A busy travel season will always favor the first clock, because its results are visible and the deadlines are real.

Gibson and Birkinshaw's version fits a one-person office best. When there is no separate unit, the ambidexterity has to live inside one person's calendar. That means deciding the size of the exploring clock up front, for example the vendor's smallest test at $2,400, and protecting a named block of time for it, instead of letting it get whatever is left over.

Two clocks, one supply of time, money, and attention. Deciding the split on purpose beats letting the louder clock decide it.

3. Leading change as a story (Lewin, Kotter, Beer and Nohria, Weick, Edmondson)

Moving from Marketing Cloud to Slate is a change effort, even with a team of one. Run Kotter's list against it. Urgency is the old process being manual and split across tools. The short-term win is the first event that runs fully through Slate. The risk is declaring victory too soon, switching off the old spreadsheet and email process before the new one has been trusted through a full cycle.

Beer and Nohria fit too. The move is partly Theory E (one system, less manual work) and partly Theory O (building the office's own skill with the tool), and a plan that sold only the first would stall. Edmondson's hospital study is the closest match to what is already happening. The teams that adopted a new technology well went through preparation, trials, and reflection, and built enough safety that people would try the new routine. Running the old process alongside the new one is a trial phase. What is still needed is the reflection step: a set date to decide what the trial proved and when the old process ends.

Weick's sensemaking point is for the people downstream. The change will be described to CC contacts, prospects, and colleagues as a story, and someone has to decide what the story is.

The overlap is the trial. The gate is where the old way ends, and it should be defined before the new way is trusted.

4. Resistance as information (Petriglieri, Piderit)

This is the thinnest idea for real examples. It is built from where resistance could show up, not from anything that has happened yet. Three places to look:

Prospective students are working nurses. A nurse who gets a sequence of automated emails may feel handled as a segment instead of seen as a person with a particular situation. That is a hypothesis about identity, not a finding.

Piderit's ambivalence gives a better reading than "unresponsive." A prospect who opens every email and never replies may want the program and dread the workload. That person is not saying no.

CC partners are the second group. Their relationship runs through a person and the Boise State brand. A change in who emails them, or how often, can read as a change in how much they matter.

Ambivalence is the common response. Reading it as a simple yes or no throws away what it is telling you.

5. What a system does to the work: the six Rs (Kellogg, Dietvorst, Lebovitz, Longoni)

Kellogg's six Rs make a quick scan of the tools in use. The table in the case study below runs it. The point of the scan is not that anything is wrong. It is that record and rate are the two Rs people tend to live under without naming.

Dietvorst's 2018 finding gives the practical fix: letting people adjust the output of an automated system, even slightly, restores trust. Here that would mean letting an advisor or a CC contact tweak the template or timing, instead of receiving a fixed sequence. Lebovitz adds that experts who feel competent use a system as a learning tool, while threatened ones defer to it or reject it. Longoni's finding that patients resist AI because it can't hold their unique situation is about medical diagnosis, so applying it to recruiting email is a stretch. It stays here only as a caution.

Six things a system can do to work. The goal is to name which ones a tool is running, not to avoid tools.

What we cut, and why

Mintzberg's five configurations and Miller's strategy-structure types describe whole organizations, and the unit here is one program and one person. Chandler's multidivisional form and the matrix and divisional charts are cut because there are no divisions to design, though Chandler's principle stays. Burns and Stalker are covered by the fit idea and need a bigger organization to show up. Humanocracy and Haier argue for removing layers of management, and there are no layers in a one-person office.

The AI-organization readings (Fountaine, Cram, Raisch and Krakowski, Brynjolfsson and McAfee) are cut because Boise State already provides its own AI tool and the reorganization lag was covered in Week 6. The Klarna reversal is about replacing customer service staff with AI, and nothing in this office is being replaced. Barley's CT scanner study and Lee's gig-work study both need a workforce to compare. Oreg and Jost are cut because labeling colleagues by disposition would be guesswork. Morrison on employee voice and the worker voice survey both need many employees. Barrett and Kiritchenko cover emotion and sentiment systems, and no tool like that is in use. Gioia and Chittipeddi, Beer, Eisenstat and Spector, and Jussupow's review are folded into the ideas above.

The case study

The plan is set. Can the office run it?

What has to change inside a one-person recruiting office for the Week 10 plan to actually run, and in what order.

What's on the table. The Week 10 plan has three tiers, a $166 bar, and three hidden dependencies. Running it takes a measure, a follow-up process on Slate, and protected time to test.

Two clocks, written down. Using what works: Idaho CC events, partner relationships, info sessions, Free Application Days. Searching for what's next: the list test, near-tier ads, Slate. A rule that fits a one-person office is to set the testing budget first (the $2,400 test is the natural size) and block a fixed amount of time for it. Trips outside Idaho have to beat $166 per engaged lead.

Moving to Slate, step by step.

  1. Keep running the old process in parallel until the first full cycle has gone through Slate.
  2. Write down in advance what "done" means before turning the old process off, so victory isn't declared early.
  3. Treat the first event that runs end to end in Slate as the first win, and tell CC partners what changed and why.
  4. Set a date to look back and decide what the trial showed.

Six Rs scan of the current tools. Only what is known is filled in. The rest still needs mapping as the tools get configured.

Who might push back, and what they might be protecting. Prospects who are working nurses (being treated as individuals), CC partners (their relationship with the school), and anyone who relies on the old spreadsheet outputs (a process they trust). These are questions to check, not findings. The cheap step is to ask each group one question before the switch, and to give them a way to adjust something small.

What the one-person limit decides. With one person, the office can't run a separate pilot team, so the order matters. Finish the Slate move before opening the next test, or the two clocks will crowd each other in the busiest part of the year.
