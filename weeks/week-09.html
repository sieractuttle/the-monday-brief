Who Could Decide to Become You

Pipes, platforms, and ecosystems, and what it means for a recruitment program that depends on Google, directory sites, and a data vendor for distribution it doesn't own.

How platforms actually work

Most businesses are what's called a pipe: value moves in one direction, a supplier to a firm to a customer, each step adding something along the way. A platform is different. It doesn't make the product, it mediates an exchange between two or more sides it doesn't fully control, and it wins by orchestrating resources it doesn't own rather than owning them itself.

That creates a specific kind of market, a two-sided one, where two distinct groups each need the other to show up, and neither shows up first on its own. The platform has to decide who pays and who gets subsidized to get both sides in the room at once, a design choice, not something that just happens. Once both sides are there, network effects can kick in: the product gets more valuable as more people use it, and small early leads can compound into one side winning the whole market.

Zoom out further and a platform sits inside something bigger: an ecosystem of everyone who built something around it, apps, sellers, partners, developers. How the platform treats that ecosystem matters. A keystone shares enough value that the things built on it keep investing and growing. A dominator extracts value until the things around it stop investing, which eventually hurts the platform too. And because a platform can change its own rules unilaterally, governance, how open or closed it is, becomes a live, ongoing design question, not something it decides once.

The sharpest version of all of this: a platform can watch what works on top of it and then do that thing itself, bundling its own version into the base it already owns. Distribution beats a better product in that fight, because the platform already has everyone's attention. The uncomfortable question for anyone building on someone else's platform is simply whether that's ever going to happen to them, and what to do before it does.

Applied to the RN-BS program

Pipes versus platforms

The RN-BS program is a pipe trying to get noticed inside several platforms it doesn't own: Google's search results, nursing-program directory sites, and the contact-list vendor from two weeks ago. None of those are the program's infrastructure, they're borrowed distribution.

A pipe moves value in a line. A platform sits in the middle, mediating sides it doesn't control, the program is one spoke among several.

Two-sided markets, and who gets subsidized

Google Ads is a two-sided market: advertisers like the School of Nursing pay, searchers get a free result. Who pays and who's subsidized is Google's design choice, and nursing directory sites often work the same way, free browsing for students, paid placement for programs.

Two sides, one priced, one subsidized. Which side pays is the platform's decision, not a market accident.

Keystone versus dominator

A keystone shares enough value that what's built on it keeps investing. A dominator extracts until it stops. This cuts two ways worth naming: whether Boise State's central marketing structure acts as a keystone or a dominator toward the School of Nursing, and whether Boise State acts as a keystone or a dominator toward its community college partners.

Where a relationship currently sits on the spectrum, and which direction it could move.

Envelopment, the actual risk

A platform eats an adjacent market by bundling its own version into the base it already owns, distribution beating a better product. A directory site that starts answering "which program is right for me" directly on its own page, instead of sending the click through, is doing the same thing to individual program pages that a bigger platform does to a smaller business built on top of it.

The bigger circle slowly answers the question the smaller one used to be needed for.

Governance, and the real defense

There's no clean fix to the fact that a platform can change its own rules. The defensible moves are: keep the direct relationship with the actual person, don't depend on one single platform for everything, move faster than the platform's own roadmap, and when possible pick platforms that need your business as much as you need their reach.

The four defensive moves, and an honest read on where the program stands on each one.

What we cut, and why

The formal two-sided market economics (Armstrong) and the deeper network-effects lock-in literature (Arthur, Farrell & Saloner, Liebowitz & Margolis) are cut because they're built for markets fighting over which platform tips to winner-take-all, not a single program's relationship to platforms that already exist. The deeper ecosystem-architecture literature (Adner, Jacobides, Cennamo & Santalo, Gawer & Cusumano) is cut for the same reason as prior weeks, it's built for firms designing an ecosystem, not a program operating inside one someone else built.

The case study

Who could decide to become you

Google, a directory site, and a data vendor, each one borrowed distribution the program doesn't control.

What's actually playing platform in this story. Three things the program depends on but doesn't control: Google, where ad rules and search-result design can change without notice; any nursing-program directory site the program is listed on; and the contact-list vendor, whose data isn't exclusive, since any competing nursing program can buy the same list.

The envelopment risk, made concrete. If a directory site ever decided to answer "which RN-BS program is right for me" directly on its own page instead of sending traffic through, that's the program losing the one thing it actually has, the direct relationship, without ever getting a vote in the decision.

Is this a market failure, or just the cost of the distribution? It's the cost. Nobody is forcing the program onto Google, a directory site, or a data vendor, and each one hands the program reach it couldn't build alone. The real question isn't whether these platforms have the right to change their own rules. It's whether the program is capturing enough value from being on them to justify staying if they do.

What the actual defense looks like. Of the four defensive moves, the program already has the strongest version of the first one: the trust built with CC advisors and students at in-person events doesn't live on Google, a directory site, or a vendor's list, and can't be absorbed the way a listing or a ranking can. Multi-homing is the second move already partly in place, running trips, Google Ads, and a list test as separate, comparable channels rather than betting everything on one.
