Who Checked That Before It Went Out?

What governance looks like for a recruiting office where one person writes, schedules, and sends most of what the program says under the Boise State name.

How governance works

Governance starts with a split. Once the people who own a business are not the people who run it, owners have to rely on managers who know more than they can check and who may want different things. Jensen and Meckling call the cost of that gap agency cost. Eisenhardt names its two causes: the agent holds information the principal can't verify, and the agent's interests don't line up automatically. Holmstrom adds the practical twist. When you can't watch what someone does, you pay attention to what you can see, and people adjust their behavior to that.

The usual fixes are boards, pay tied to performance, auditors, and the threat of a takeover. Fama and Jensen separate managing decisions from controlling them, and boards exist for the second job. They fail in a predictable way. Westphal and Zajac show that boards get captured by the executives they are supposed to check, partly through who gets picked as a director. Hillman and Dalziel add that boards also supply advice and resources, so the people checking you are also people you depend on. A structure can meet every requirement and still miss whatever it was never set up to look for.

Who the checks protect depends on what the firm is for. Freeman's answer is that a firm owes something to everyone it affects or is affected by, not only its owners. Donaldson and Preston split that into three claims: who actually affects the firm, who it pays to treat well, and who it owes. Jones presses the hard question. If you treat people well because it pays, is that ethics or strategy?

Firms also answer to rules they didn't write. Baron's point is that market strategy and nonmarket strategy have to fit, and Hillman and Hitt separate one-off political moves from standing relationships. North calls institutions the rules of the game, formal and informal. Meyer and Rowan add that organizations adopt formal structures to look legitimate, and those structures can drift away from what people actually do. Suchman says legitimacy is much easier to lose than to rebuild.

The same gap shows up in algorithms. A model learns from labels, and a label is a stand-in for what you really care about. Obermeyer and colleagues found a widely used health-risk algorithm underestimated Black patients' needs because it used healthcare cost as a stand-in for need, and cost reflected unequal access to care. Barocas and Selbst show that neutral-looking data practices can produce discriminatory results without anyone intending it. Mittelstadt and colleagues point out that questions like fairness and opacity never show up in an accuracy score. Calo lists the levers regulators really have: disclosure, liability, procurement, and certification.

Taken together, each of these asks the same question of any control: what can it see, and who decided what counts as seen?

The ideas this chapter runs on

Applied to the RN-BS program

1. Agents and what the principal can't see (Jensen and Meckling, Eisenhardt, Holmstrom)

The recruiter is the program's agent in public. That means writing ads, running email journeys, posting to Facebook, Instagram, and LinkedIn, and pitching at transfer fairs. The platforms are agents too. Google Ads and Meta spend the budget on the program's behalf, and Slate and Marketing Cloud send on its behalf. The people who answer for the program's reputation can't watch most of that directly. They see the monthly social and ad reports, registration counts, and what the recruiter tells them.

Holmstrom's point is that when quality is hard to observe, people end up judged on what is easy to count. Impressions, clicks, and registrations are easy to count. Applications and enrollments are what matter. And because one person owns lead follow-up, that person holds information about what happens after the first click that nobody else sees.

The less an owner can verify, the more the owner relies on what the agent chooses to show.

2. Deciding versus checking (Fama and Jensen, Westphal and Zajac, Hillman and Dalziel)

The recruiter makes most day-to-day decisions, and the boss checks the big ones, such as approving the move to run info sessions and free application days through Slate. That is the decision-control layer, and it only works on what reaches it. In a one-person office, capture is structural, not a matter of intent. Whoever writes the report chooses what it shows.

Hillman and Dalziel's resource point applies as well. The boss and the university's central marketing are both checkers and the source of the program's support, which makes pushing back costly. Reports that show the same measures the same way every month, including the weak months, are the cheapest protection.

Control only sees what the report lets through. Whoever writes the report decides what that is.

3. Who a claim can hurt (Freeman, Donaldson and Preston, Jones)

Every claim in an ad, email, or event pitch lands on someone: a working RN planning life around the program, a CC advisor repeating the pitch to students, a faculty member who has to deliver what was promised. The three versions of stakeholder theory sort the tension. Descriptive: the nurse who rearranges a work schedule is clearly affected. Instrumental: accurate claims cut down on drop-off and complaints. Normative: working nurses are owed an accurate picture.

Several facts were checked against the program's FAQ page. It is 100% online, there are no traditional clinical hours (students complete two experiential learning project courses), there is one tuition rate with no out-of-state tuition and an average total cost of about $12,120, and students finish in about 18 months on average at 6 credits per semester year-round, with faster or slower paces possible. Jones's question is whether a claim that accurate would survive if a rounder, more attractive number converted better.

The three tags are three different claims on the same web. Which one is being argued changes the answer.

4. Rules written elsewhere (Baron, Hillman and Hitt, North, Meyer and Rowan, Suchman)

Many rules that govern the program's marketing are written by others. Boise State participates in the State Authorization Reciprocity Agreement, and its eCampus Center handles state authorization. That comes from the university's own page, which does not mention nursing specifically. Partner colleges set their own terms for what outside recruiters can do at their events, and what those terms are for CWI and Lewis-Clark State has not been confirmed.

The recruiter's own nonmarket work is relational, the standing relationships with CC advisors that keep events on the calendar. Meyer and Rowan's warning is that formal structure can drift from practice. Approved language and consent rules can exist on paper while the real work in a one-person office runs on habit. Suchman's legitimacy is the reason that matters. Trust with advisors and hospital employers is slow to build and quick to lose.

Most rules come from the outer rings. The risk is the gap between the paper version and the practice.

5. The label inside the platform (Obermeyer, Barocas and Selbst, Mittelstadt, Calo)

Every ad platform optimizes toward a label. A registration for a free application day is a stand-in for intent, but it is not an application. The platform learns who registers, not who applies. That is the same shape as Obermeyer's finding, where cost stood in for need. Google Ads learns from whichever action it is told counts, and Meta's delivery is also optimized by the platform.

A study outside this week's reading list (Ali and colleagues, 2019) showed Facebook's delivery skewed by gender and race on real employment and housing ads even with neutral targeting. That was not education advertising, so it is an analogy, not a finding. If Slate lead scoring is turned on and learns from past enrollees, it will favor the people the program already reaches. The legal rules in these readings concern hiring and credit, and whether they apply to education marketing has not been checked, so this is an ethics and reputation point. Calo's disclosure lever is the practical one: ask each platform what it is optimizing for.

A registration stands in for intent the way cost stood in for need. The model only ever sees the stand-in.

What we cut, and why

Berle and Means, Jensen and Ruback, Shleifer and Vishny, Jensen and Murphy, and Core and colleagues cover ownership, takeovers, and CEO pay, and a program has no shareholders or takeover threat. Jensen's free cash flow paper is cut because there is no evidence it applies to this budget. The board-specific papers (Zahra and Pearce, Hambrick and Jackson, Finkelstein and D'Aveni, and Westphal's board games paper) are covered by the oversight idea. Olson, Stigler, and Bonardi, Hillman and Keim concern coalitions and investing in political markets, and a program has no lobbying budget or coalition to build.

DiMaggio and Powell and Deephouse are cut because checking whether nursing programs' copy looks alike would take an audit of competitor pages that hasn't been done. Carroll, McWilliams and Siegel, and Margolis and Walsh are about company-wide CSR spending. Buolamwini and Gebru and Kleinberg and colleagues cover facial recognition and bail decisions, which are far from recruiting, and Obermeyer makes the same point closer to home. The measurement paper from the algorithmic-bias set is cut because its accuracy-versus-validity point comes through Obermeyer's proxy-label example. The EU AI Act, Smuha, Dafoe, Floridi and colleagues, and Engler are cut because European rules don't bind a US program's marketing, and Boise State already provides its own AI tool and governs its use. The class case and exercises from the slides are left out so the chapter stands on the readings.

The case study

Who checked that before it went out?

How much checking can a one-person recruiting office add without slowing the work?

What's on the table. One person writes, schedules, and sends nearly everything the program says in public. The question is how much checking an office this size can realistically add.

A claims ledger. Start with what the program publishes, then check where each claim shows up. The last column is for the office to fill in.

Who can see what, and one cheap check for each.

Checks scaled to a one-person office

RelationshipWhat the principal can't easily seeA cheap check
Leadership and the recruiterWhat happens to a lead after the first clickOne monthly report with the same measures every time, weak months included
Program and ad platformsWho sees the ads, and whyCompare who the ads reach by state, credential, and source against who is eligible

The registered, attended, applied ladder. For each campaign, write down which of the three it counts, then look at who drops out at each step by credential, state, and source. If one group registers and never applies, the label is hiding a problem.

A ten-minute check before anything new goes out. Does each claim trace to a published page? Is the audience what was meant? Does anything about sender or consent rules need confirming with the university? Would a second reader catch something? A second reader only needs to see new claims, not every post.

Know where the rules live. State authorization has its own office at Boise State. The job is to know where the answer lives and cite it, not to re-decide it each time.

What the one-person limit decides. A committee isn't realistic. A short check before sending, a monthly look-back, and one second reader for new claims is.
