Week 08

Rent the List or Buy It Outright: What M&A Theory Says About a Nursing Data Deal

A real vendor quote for a nursing contact list, run through the same logic that explains why most corporate acquisitions overpay for synergies that haven't been earned yet.

The ideas this chapter runs on

Why firms have boundaries at all (Coase, Williamson). The RN-BS program currently builds its lead pipeline largely through in-person recruiting, five trips so far, $5,814.08 spent, 35 leads. A nursing data vendor is now offering two very different ways to buy into a much larger pool: renting access to a filtered list of RN, LPN, LVN and CNA contacts through a managed email campaign the vendor runs, or licensing the file outright to own and run from Boise State's own tools. Those are two different governance forms for the same underlying resource, and transaction cost theory says the right one depends on how much you trust the asset you can't fully inspect before you pay for it.

Ally or acquire, applied to a data vendor instead of a company (Dyer, Kale & Singh). Renting the list through a managed campaign is the lower-commitment, alliance-style option: the vendor filters by credential and state, writes and sends the emails, and hands back only the nurses who opened or clicked, meaning the program only follows up with people who showed real interest. Licensing the file outright, $2,000 for the smallest slice up to $60,000 for the BS-DNP nationwide file, is closer to an acquisition: full ownership, a large upfront cost, and the assumption that the program can run effective outreach to a cold list using tools it already has.

The M&A empirical record, and why reach isn't the same as value (Sirower; King, Dalton, Daily & Covin). The vendor's own numbers show a familiar shape: cost per send drops as volume goes up, $900 per send at 6 campaigns down to $450 at 24, which is exactly the kind of scale argument that makes a bigger buy look more efficient on paper. But Sirower's core finding about corporate acquisitions applies directly here: deals get justified by counting the value of scale before it's actually been earned. Reaching 30,911 nurses nationwide is not the same thing as converting them, and the vendor's own quote gives reach numbers and campaign costs, not a single projected lead or enrollment figure. The recruiting trips have produced 35 leads for $5,814.08 spent so far, about $166 per lead, a real, measured cost. The list numbers aren't comparable yet because no one has measured what a cold nursing contact, even a filtered, credentialed one, actually converts at.

The relational view says the trips might be buying something a list can't (Dyer & Singh). Recruiting events work through relationship-specific trust, the CC advisor who knows the program, the student who talked to a real person at a table. A purchased list, even with a 90% accuracy guarantee and free replacement of bad contacts, has none of that built in. The guarantee covers whether an email address is correct, not whether that nurse has any reason to trust an email from a school they've never interacted with.

Real options and staged commitment (Kogut). This is the frame that actually resolves the decision. The vendor's own menu has a built-in option structure: Option 1 (6 campaigns) is a cheap, short test of which audience responds, before the program commits to Option 2 or 3, let alone a $60,000 outright license. Buying the full nationwide license before running a single test campaign is paying for the acquisition before buying the option to learn whether it's worth acquiring.

What we cut, and why

The detailed acquisition-integration literature (Jemison & Sitkin, Pablo, Zollo & Singh, Graebner) is cut because it's built for absorbing another company's staff and systems, which has no equivalent in a list purchase. The property-rights extension of the theory of the firm (Grossman & Hart, Hart & Moore) is cut because it repeats ground covered in an earlier chapter. The AI lens on ally-or-acquire is cut for the same reason as prior weeks, it's a university-level question, already settled by Boise State's own AI tool.

The case study

Rent the list, or buy it outright

A real vendor quote for RN-BS nursing data, tested against what five recruiting trips have actually cost.

What's actually on the table. A vendor is offering RN-BS data at two tiers, Western states (5,766 contacts) or nationwide (30,911 contacts), in two forms: a managed email campaign that rents the data and runs the outreach (6, 12, or 24 sends, $2,400 to $10,800 depending on tier and volume), or an outright license to own the file and run outreach independently ($2,000 to $7,500 for RN-BS alone, far more for the broader BS-DNP audience).

Why "we'd reach so many more nurses" isn't yet an answer. Recruiting trips so far have cost $5,814.08 and produced 35 leads, a real number at roughly $166 per lead. The list's reach numbers, 5,766 to 30,911 nurses depending on tier, are not leads. They're the size of an inbox list that has not yet been tested against this program's actual pitch. Treating reach as a substitute for leads is the same move Sirower's research warns against in corporate acquisitions: the value gets counted at the size of the opportunity, not at what's actually been demonstrated. At $166 per lead, the trips are already a reasonably efficient channel, which raises the bar for what the list purchase needs to prove, not lowers it.

What the staged option actually looks like. Rather than choosing between continuing the trips as-is or committing to a large list purchase, the real-options framing points to running the cheapest managed-campaign tier, RN-BS West, Option 1, $2,400 for 6 campaigns, as a deliberate test. That's less than half of what's been spent on trips so far, and because the vendor returns which nurses opened or clicked, it produces an actual comparable number: a cost-per-engaged-lead figure that can be set directly against the $166 figure from the trips. Only once that number exists does it make sense to decide whether to scale to Option 2 or 3, expand to the nationwide tier, or consider licensing the file outright.

What this doesn't replace. Even a successful test doesn't make the CC and recruiting-event relationships redundant. The relational view's point stands: a nurse who meets a real person at an event and a nurse who opens a cold email are not operating from the same level of trust in the program, and the eventual mix of channels should reflect that, not just whichever one tests cheaper per lead.
