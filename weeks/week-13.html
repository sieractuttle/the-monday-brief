How strategy works when the future is unknown

Frank Knight's old split does a lot of work here. Risk is the part of the future you can price, because you know the odds, so you can insure against it or budget for it. Uncertainty is the part where nobody knows the odds because the thing hasn't happened before. Keynes made the same point and added that when the odds can't be known, people lean on convention. They assume the future looks like the recent past, and that shared assumption acts as a coordination device. Taleb adds the tail. The events that move history sit in the part of the distribution that normal-curve thinking leaves out.

Tetlock's decades of scoring forecasters gives the practical correction. Confident experts with one big idea did worse than humble ones who kept updating, and the better forecasters wrote down specific predictions, attached probabilities, and revised in small steps when new evidence arrived.

Scenario planning is the toolkit for the uncertain part. Wack's insight at Shell was not better prediction. It was changing how managers saw, so they weren't blindsided. Schoemaker made it a method: build a few different, internally consistent stories, then test your decisions against all of them. Courtney, Kirkland, and Viguerie add a map. You are never at zero uncertainty or in total fog. You sit at one of four levels: a clear-enough future, a few alternate futures, a range, or true ambiguity. The level dictates the posture, which is to shape the future, adapt to it, or reserve the right to play.

Real options reasoning treats uncertainty as something you can use. Kogut showed that alliances and joint ventures work as options, a small position that lets you learn before you commit. Dixit and Pindyck explain why. When a commitment is irreversible and the future is uncertain, the option to wait has value. McGrath adds that a small failed bet is information bought at a known price. Adner and Levinthal give the caution. It only works when you can actually stage the commitment, actually learn from it, and actually walk away.

The AI readings add a way to look at the technology without guessing the far future. Autor and colleagues sort work by task, routine versus non-routine, and Acemoglu and Restrepo show that automation takes people out of some tasks while new tasks pull them back in. Which force wins is an empirical question. Acemoglu's 2024 estimate for the first wave is modest, while other sources in the set expect far more, and the gap between them is itself a sign of how uncertain this is. Brynjolfsson and Mitchell give a rubric for which tasks machine learning can take and which it can't. The strategic question for any unit is what AI can actually do on its own tasks this quarter.

The ideas this chapter runs on

Applied to the RN-BS program

1. Risk versus uncertainty (Knight, Keynes, Taleb)

Some of the program's numbers have a history and can be treated as risk. Registrations for a free application day, cost per click on Google Ads, and how many events partner colleges have hosted in past years are all things with a record. Other questions have no record at all. How working nurses will find programs in five years, and what a BSN will be worth to them, are uncertainty.

Keynes's point shows up in the budget. Planning the next year from last year's results is a convention, and it works until the ground moves. Taleb's tail is a dependency failing without warning. The program has high-priority events and partner colleges that bring in many students, and that concentration is the exposure. If one of them hosts less, cuts back, or changes terms, the result is a big drop from one source, not a little less of everything. No useful odds exist for that, so it is better handled with backups than with a forecast.

The first question to ask of any number is whether the odds can be written down. If they can't, the plan changes.

2. Forecasting well anyway (Tetlock and Gardner)

A recruiting office makes forecasts all the time, such as how many people will register for the next info session or whether a partner college will host in spring, and almost never scores them. Tetlock's practice is cheap. Write the question, a probability or a range, and a date. Check the outcome, then adjust in small steps. Over a year, that is a record of where the office's instincts are good and where they are not.

Forecasters who update in small steps beat confident ones who don't. The habit is cheap, and the record builds over time.

3. Scenarios and the four levels (Wack, Schoemaker, Courtney et al., van der Heijden)

The useful scenarios for this office are about how prospects find and choose a program. These are drafts for planning, not facts or forecasts:

Search stays the front door. Google Ads and partner college events keep carrying most inquiries. Assistants become the front door. People ask an AI assistant which RN-to-BSN program fits, and the answer is built from published pages, so the program's own facts matter more than its ad copy. Employers and partner colleges steer. Hospitals and colleges point nurses to preferred programs, so those relationships matter more than ads.

Courtney's levels sort the channels. Email through Slate and Google Ads over the next year are close to a clear-enough future, because results show up monthly. Partner college events and Meta ads are alternate futures. How prospects will find programs in five years is a range at best.

The level of uncertainty decides the posture. The common mistake is treating a range as if it were a clear forecast.

4. Real options and staged commitment (Kogut, Dixit and Pindyck, McGrath, Adner and Levinthal)

For the range-level questions, the posture is to reserve the right to play. That means small commitments that teach something and can be stopped. Adner and Levinthal's tests are whether it can be staged, whether it will teach something, and whether it can be walked away from. A short capped test on a Meta campaign passes all three. A multi-year commitment to a single channel or vendor, sized to today's channel mix, fails them, because it only pays in one scenario.

McGrath's point matters for a small office. A test that fails cheaply is information, and nobody should treat it as wasted budget.

Each step teaches something before the next one. A commitment that fails the three tests is a bet, not an option.

5. Tasks, not jobs (Autor, Acemoglu and Restrepo, Acemoglu, Brynjolfsson and Mitchell)

Looking at the recruiter's job task by task is more useful than asking whether AI will replace it. Boise State provides its own AI tool, so the question is what that tool can do on these tasks. Drafting ad and email variants and summarizing monthly reports are routine and cognitive, so a tool can take a first pass, and a person still checks the claims and the numbers. Talking with a partner college advisor, or with a nurse deciding whether the program fits her life, is particular and relational, and no tool takes that over. Deciding which events and channels to cut is judgment under uncertainty and stays with the person and the boss.

Acemoglu and Restrepo's other half also shows up. New tasks appear, such as checking AI drafts and keeping the claims ledger current.

Tasks leave, and new tasks arrive. The useful question is which tasks, in which cell of the grid.

What we cut, and why

The AGI, alignment, and catastrophic risk readings (Bostrom, Russell, Turing, Good, Bengio and colleagues, Hendrycks, Gabriel, Aschenbrenner) are cut because a recruiting office has no decision to make about them, and Aschenbrenner is speculative by the reading list's own description. Company positioning from AI developers (Anthropic, OpenAI, Altman, Dafoe, Amodei's essay) concerns firms that build AI, and the program is a user. The economy-wide labor readings (Autor 2015, Autor 2024, the 2019 Acemoglu and Restrepo paper, D. Susskind) repeat the task idea at a scale the program can't act on. Susskind and Susskind on the professions would need data on what working nurses will need from a BSN, which isn't available here.

The long-view readings (Bresnahan and Trajtenberg, David, Perez, Gordon, Mokyr, Schumpeter, Goldin, Postman) are cut because the J-curve idea was covered in Week 6 and the rest are economy-wide. Brynjolfsson and McAfee, LeCun and colleagues, Marcus and Davis, and Mitchell are background on the technology, and Brynjolfsson and Mitchell carry the practical point. Trigeorgis and the valuation math are cut because nobody needs to compute a strike price to decide whether to run a small test. The class case, game, and exercises from the slides are left out so the chapter stands on the readings.

The case study

Which door will they use?

The biggest question in the next year of recruitment can't be priced: how working nurses will find programs over the next five years.

What's on the table. The RN-BS program is planning the next year of recruitment. Some channels can be planned from results. One question, how prospects will find programs in five years, can't be.

Signals to watch. Review the answers to the "how did you find us" question by source and over time, along with clicks and cost per click in the Google Ads reports and how many partner college events happen each year.

A commitment to avoid. Any multi-year commitment to one channel or vendor that only pays if search stays the front door.

A forecast log. Write down the question, a probability or range, and a date. For example: how many people will register for the next info session, and whether a given partner college will host in spring. Check each one when it resolves.

Where the task view lands. Use the university's AI tool for first drafts and report summaries, and keep a person on claims, numbers, and relationships. Add the checking to the job as a named task.

What the one-person limit decides. Pick two of the four bets for next quarter, not all four.

Position. The recruiter's job is not to predict which door nurses will use in 2030. It is to keep the program accurate and findable through all three, and to spend small until the signals show which one is real.
