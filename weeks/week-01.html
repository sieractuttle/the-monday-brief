Week 01

Strategy & Strategic Leadership

The one idea a higher ed marketer actually needs from this week's reading, and what would be a mistake to leave out.

The one idea

Strategy is not a list of activities. Hambrick and Fredrickson call this out directly: when someone says "our strategy is unrivaled customer service" or "our strategy is a global expansion," that's a strategic thread, not a strategy. A real strategy answers five linked questions: where will you compete (arenas), how will you get there (vehicles), how will you win once you're there (differentiators), what's the sequence (staging), and how does it make money (economic logic).

Porter's piece backs this up from a different angle. Operational effectiveness, doing what everyone else does but better, is not strategy either. Strategy is a specific, defensible position built on trade-offs, on choosing what not to do.

For a nursing enrollment marketer, this matters because it's easy to mistake "we ran a paid social campaign, an open house, and an email nurture sequence" for having a strategy. Those are vehicles. The real strategic question underneath them is sharper: among pre-licensure, RN-to-BS, and BS-to-DNP, which arena are you actually trying to win, against which specific competitor programs, with what differentiator that isn't just "great faculty" or "great outcomes," and what's the sequence, meaning what do you build first so the next thing compounds instead of resetting to zero every cycle.

What would be wrong to leave out

The purpose research, specifically Gartenberg, Prat, and Serafeim. It's the empirical companion to Bartlett and Ghoshal's "beyond strategy to purpose" argument, and it complicates it in a useful way. They found purpose alone doesn't correlate with performance. What correlates is purpose plus clarity, and critically, that effect is driven by how middle management and professional staff perceive it, not senior leadership and not frontline hourly workers. That's the layer this research says matters most for enrollment teams. A mission statement on the university website doesn't do anything by itself. What moves outcomes is whether the people running recruitment can articulate a clear, specific version of why the program exists and who it's for, and act on that clarity consistently.

One more thing worth not skipping: Mintzberg's deliberate vs. emergent strategy work. A lot of real strategy gets discovered by noticing what's already working in practice and doubling down, rather than being fully planned in advance. That maps onto testing content with a student feedback group before publishing. That's not just good marketing hygiene, it's literally how emergent strategy gets formed.

This week's field notes

Five things to do Monday

01 / 05 Field note

Your campaign isn't a strategy. It's one piece of one.

Why

A real strategy answers five questions: arenas, vehicles, differentiators, staging, economic logic. A social push or an email series is just the vehicle. — Hambrick & Fredrickson

Do Monday

Map your current nursing recruitment plan onto the five parts. If "differentiators" and "economic logic" are blank, fix that before adding another channel.

02 / 05 Field note

Pick the applicant you're willing to lose this cycle.

Why

Real advantage comes from trade-offs, not doing more of everything. One message aimed at every applicant type wins none of them. — Porter

Do Monday

Rank pre-licensure, RN-to-BS, and BS-to-DNP. Give one a sharp, differentiated push this cycle. Let the other two run on autopilot.

03 / 05 Field note

A mission statement doesn't move applicants. Clarity does.

Why

Purpose only predicts performance when it's paired with clarity, and that effect runs through mid-level staff, not leadership. That's you. — Gartenberg, Prat & Serafeim

Do Monday

Ask two teammates to finish this sentence: "Choose us over [nearest competitor] because ___." If the answers don't match, fix that before writing new copy.

04 / 05 Field note

Stop planning the whole calendar. Follow what already worked.

Why

The strongest strategies are often found in what's already working, not fully planned in advance. — Mintzberg

Do Monday

Pull last month's top-performing post or email. Name the one reason it worked. Build this week's content around repeating that, not filling a grid.

05 / 05 Field note

Write the strategy before you build the deck.

Why

Polished slides can quietly stand in for real strategic thinking. Producing a deck isn't the same as making a strategy. — Kaplan

Do Monday

Before your next stakeholder meeting, write the plan as one plain paragraph. If it doesn't hold up in prose, more slides won't save it.
