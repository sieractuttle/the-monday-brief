Every State Line Is a Border

Why firms go abroad, what distance costs them, and what it means for an online nursing program that crosses state lines.

How going abroad works

Going international is a bet that an advantage you built at home is worth more somewhere else than it costs to carry there. Dunning's test says that bet only makes sense when three things line up at once: you own something worth exporting (a brand, a technology, a routine), the new location offers something home doesn't, and running it yourself beats licensing it or selling it through someone else.

Firms rarely make that bet all at once. They usually enter close markets first, learn, then move to distant ones, and they pay a penalty along the way: a foreign entrant carries costs a local incumbent doesn't. The newer view is that the real penalty is being outside the right network, not just being foreign.

Two pulls shape every structure. One says be efficient and consistent everywhere. The other says fit each local market. Few firms can do both well. Ghemawat's CAGE framework puts a price on distance by splitting it into four kinds: cultural, administrative, geographic, and economic. They don't move together, so a market can be close in miles and still hard to reach. His broader point is that most firms are regional rather than global, because distance never fully disappears.

How you enter is a trade between control and commitment. Exporting is fast and flexible but hands off control. A wholly owned operation gives full control and full exposure. Underneath all of it sit institutions, the formal and informal rules that make a market work. Strategies that quietly assume a rule exists at home often fail where it doesn't.

The ideas this chapter runs on

Applied to the RN-BS program

Why go beyond Idaho (Dunning's OLI test)

Ownership: the Boise State name, a 70-year nursing history, and flat-rate tuition with no out-of-state premium. Location: for an online program, location means where reachable RNs without a BSN are, not where the program is delivered. Internalization: running outreach yourself (trips, your own email) versus renting reach from a vendor, the same make-or-buy question from Weeks 7 and 8. The test only passes if all three hold, so a big list alone doesn't justify the trip.

A firm goes abroad only where all three circles meet. A large list alone covers one circle, not three.

How the program goes: stages, outsidership, and entry mode (Uppsala, Zaheer, Johanson and Vahlne)

An online program with no out-of-state tuition can enroll anyone from day one, like a born-global firm. The trust network can't. In Idaho, CC advisors know Boise State. In other states you start outside the network. Recruiting travel so far has cost $5,814.08 for 35 leads, about $166 each, and that is the price of building network position in places where nobody knows you. The entry-mode ladder maps cleanly: Google Ads and email are low-commitment exporting, an MOU with an out-of-state CC is an alliance, a jointly run advising touchpoint is closer to a joint venture, and a staffed presence in a state is full ownership. CCs cutting back on events shows what low control costs.

Today's mix of ads, email, and recruiting trips sits on the lower rungs. Each step up buys control and costs commitment.

Integration versus responsiveness (Prahalad and Doz, Bartlett and Ghoshal)

One online program with one price pulls hard toward a single consistent message everywhere. The local pull is real but narrower: Idaho CCs get a dual-enrollment message, while elsewhere the message is mostly generic. The vendor's plan already adapts by credential (RNs see the 12-month completion path, LPNs, LVNs and CNAs see dual enrollment), which is responsiveness by audience, not by place. With one person covering recruitment and marketing, a transnational structure isn't realistic. The practical answer is the "international" archetype: export the home model with light adaptation, and go local only where a relationship justifies it.

The top-right box is the hardest to run and needs staff the program doesn't have. Going local selectively is the realistic move.

Pricing distance (CAGE and semiglobalization)

The four distances don't move together. The vendor defines "Western states" as 13 states including Alaska and Hawaii, which is regional on a map but far apart geographically. Flat-rate tuition removes most of the economic distance a program charging out-of-state rates would face. Ghemawat and Rugman both argue firms do best regionally first. The vendor's own tiers say the same thing: 5,766 contacts in the West versus 30,911 nationwide.

Economic distance stays low everywhere because of flat-rate tuition. Administrative distance is the one to confirm state by state.

Institutions and hidden dependencies (Peng, North, Khanna and Palepu)

A strategy that assumes a rule exists is a strategy with a hidden dependency in it. For this program those are: CCs continuing to host events (already cut back once), the rules for enrolling and transferring students across state lines, and the rules for emailing a purchased list. Khanna and Palepu's "institutional void" also fits: in a far state there is no trusted intermediary who already vouches for Boise State, and a CC advisor is exactly that intermediary.

Three rules the strategy quietly depends on, each with an owner and a date so none of them stays hidden.

What we cut, and why

The foundational internalization economics (Hymer, Vernon, Buckley and Casson, Rugman) are folded into the OLI idea so the chapter doesn't relitigate transaction costs after Weeks 7 and 8. The transnational and metanational forms (Doz, Santos and Williamson) are cut because they need a multi-unit organization. Porter's global-industries framework and the national-advantage diamond are cut because they describe whole industries and countries, not one program. The emerging-markets playbook, political risk, and sovereign AI readings (Dafoe, Bradford, CSET, Smuha, Doshi and Bhatt) are cut because Boise State already provides its own AI tool, and chips, export controls and data localization aren't program-level decisions.

The case study

Every state line is a border

How far from Idaho should an online nursing program spend its effort?

What's on the table. The program can be promoted in every state, so the real decision is how far from Idaho to spend effort. Recruiting travel so far is $5,814.08 for 35 leads. The vendor's smallest test is $2,400 for 6 campaigns to 5,766 Western contacts, or $5,400 for 30,911 nationwide. GCU and WGU are already national online programs, so out of state Boise State is the entrant and they are the incumbents.

Distance, priced.

CAGE distance for RN-BS recruiting

Cultural CC advisors and hospital employers outside Idaho don't know Boise State, so trust has to be built through the brand and repeated presence.
Administrative Enrollment, transfer and email rules can differ by state, so a neighboring state is not automatically an easy one.
Geographic Travel is the cost so far, and the vendor's "West" includes Alaska and Hawaii.
Economic Flat-rate tuition removes the out-of-state price gap, the one distance where Boise State gets better as it goes farther.

Go regional first, in tiers. Home is Idaho CCs, where in-person events and a dual-enrollment message earn their cost. Near is the Western states, reached by email and Google Ads first, with a trip only where a CC relationship or event already exists. Far is everywhere else, digital only. A concrete rule: a trip outside Idaho has to beat the cost per engaged lead from the list test, with $166 as the bar to beat.

Name the hidden dependencies. Three to track: whether each partner CC keeps hosting events, whether each target state's enrollment and transfer rules are confirmed before money is spent there, and whether email rules are cleared before a list is used. Give each one an owner and a check date.

What the one-person limit decides. Because one person can't run local-responsive programs in many places, the program should be responsive in Idaho and consistent elsewhere. That is a choice, not a shortfall.
