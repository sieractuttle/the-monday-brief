Week 05

Dynamic Business-Level Strategy and Competitive Dynamics

Week 4 asked what strategy is when rivals can copy your operational moves. This is the harder version: what happens to that advantage over time, and is the window shorter than it used to be?

The ideas this chapter runs on

Not every rival responds to every move. Chen's awareness-motivation-capability framework says a competitor only reacts if they notice, care, and can act. Market commonality and resource similarity predict who that is. A giant at national scale may never notice a small regional move. A closer, similarly-sized rival sharing the same pipeline notices immediately, and reacts fast.

The same message doesn't work on every prospect. Moore's chasm and Rogers' adopter categories say a prospect who's already comparing you to a named competitor is in a different decision stage than one who hasn't started thinking about it yet. One needs a direct answer. The other needs a reason to trust you at all.

Sustainable advantage may be the wrong target. D'Aveni's hypercompetition argument and McGrath's transient advantage both say a defended position eventually gets overtaken. The discipline isn't protecting one edge forever, it's having the next one ready before the current one erodes.

In a settled market, trust beats novelty. Utterback and Abernathy's life-cycle model says a market moves from a fluid, experimental phase to a settled one where the competing options look similar. Once that happens, a genuinely rare asset, like decades of institutional history, does more work than another feature claim.

What we cut, and why

The Red Queen effect (Barnett & Hansen, Barnett & McKendrick, Derfus et al.). The argument, that competing hard against tough rivals makes an organization stronger regardless of who wins any single round, is genuinely one of the better ideas in this week's reading. We cut it because it's an argument about internal capability and culture, not something that changes what a specific pitch says to a specific prospect, which is what this chapter is built to produce.

D'Aveni's full four-arena model of hypercompetition. The escalation arenas (cost-quality, timing-know-how, strongholds, deep pockets) are a complete framework in their own right. We kept the core takeaway, that a defended position eventually gets overtaken, and cut the full model, which deserves its own piece rather than a paragraph here.

The case study

Same tools, shorter window

What happens to the RN-BS pitch once CWI notices Lewis-Clark.

The bridge from last week. Week 4 found Boise State's real edge against GCU and WGU: a dual-enrollment pathway letting ADN students start BSN coursework before finishing their associate degree. Nobody else in that comparison could say that truthfully. Idaho changes the comparison.

Act 1: the advantage has already been matched, just not by who you'd expect. Lewis-Clark State College has a standing MOU with CWI, and with CEI, NIC, and CSI, letting CWI's ADN students take BSN coursework concurrently, finishing their BSN one to two semesters after their associate degree. Same structural move Boise State made. LC State also charges no out-of-state tuition for students under 12 credits, runs a total program cost around $10,204, the lowest four-year tuition in the state, and is independently ranked #1 in Idaho for best, fastest, and most affordable RN-to-BSN. This is Chen's framework playing out directly: WGU, at national scale, likely never notices a single Idaho community college pipeline. LC State, sharing the same CWI feeder pool and comparable size, notices immediately, is motivated to defend that pipeline, and has the capability to match the move fast.

Act 2: two different pitches for two different audiences. The CWI students already asking about Lewis-Clark are Moore's early-market prospect, close, informed, comparing specifics right now. A generic "flexible online BSN" pitch does nothing for them; they need a direct answer to "why here instead of LC State," fast, before they commit. The much larger group of CWI students not yet thinking about a BSN at all is the mainstream Rogers describes, and mainstream prospects aren't won with a head-to-head competitor comparison, they're won with trust signals: how long has this program existed, who else has gone through it, does it hold up. Sending the competitive pitch to that broader group is wasted, and sending the trust pitch to a student who already has an LC State offer in hand is too slow for where they are.

Act 3: the two differentiators LC State can't touch. Once the dual-enrollment structure is a wash, the honest next question is what's actually still unmatched.

What's still unmatched, once dual enrollment is a wash

Differentiator Boise State RN-BS Lewis-Clark State
Concurrent enrollment with CWI Yes — dual enrollment pathway Yes — CWI-LC State Nursing MOU, matched
Multi-state licensure portability NC-SARA authorized in all 50 states Built around Idaho; not the selling point
Institutional history Training nurses since 1955 — Idaho's oldest nursing school still operating BSN program approved 2003

National portability. Boise State's RN-BS carries NC-SARA authorization across all 50 states. LC State's whole structure, the community college MOUs, the in-state tuition break, is built around Idaho. A CWI student planning to stay in Idaho may not care about this yet, but it's a real structural difference worth having ready for the segment that does, or might move later.

Seventy years of institutional trust. Boise State has been training nurses since 1955, Idaho's oldest nursing school still operating. Neither LC State's BSN program, GCU, nor WGU can claim anything close. In a category this settled, where the "who's fastest, who's cheapest" comparison is getting crowded fast, longevity is one of the few things genuinely hard to copy, and it's underused right now. This is the McGrath argument in practice: don't wait for the current edge to fully erode before the next one is ready. The 70-year history is available today, not hypothetical.

What this means for the pitch, concretely. For CWI students already weighing LC State: lead with the 50-state portability and put the program's history in as the credibility layer underneath it, don't lead with dual enrollment since it no longer differentiates. For the broader CWI population not yet comparing schools: lead with the 70 years and the track record, save the structural specifics for when they're closer to deciding.
