Week 07

Is the Program Better Off Inside, or Just Along for the Ride

Porter's parenting-advantage tests, and the make-or-buy question, applied to whether Boise State's centralized structure is actually earning its keep for RN-BS recruitment.

The ideas this chapter runs on

Porter's parenting-advantage framework runs on two tests. The better-off test asks whether a unit is genuinely worth more operating inside the parent than it would be outside it. The cost-of-entry test asks whether whatever it cost to bring a unit inside, or to keep it inside, has already consumed the value of being there. Most corporate strategies fail at least one of these, and the test matters because it's specific enough to actually apply, rather than a vague sense that shared resources help.

Vertical integration and make-or-buy is the applied, functional version of the same question. Williamson's transaction cost framework, Porter's Chapter 14 treatment of vertical integration as a strategic move, and Harrigan's strategy matrix all ask the same thing about a specific activity rather than a whole unit: should this be done in-house, bought from the market, or something in between. Harrigan's version is especially useful because it isn't binary. It maps out when to fully integrate, when to contract partially, and when to exit a function altogether.

The better-off test, with specifics

Is RN-BS recruitment genuinely better off running through Boise State's central marketing structure than it would be as its own autonomous function? Two concrete things point toward yes. The program inherits systems it didn't have to build: MarketingCloud as the current system of record, and Slate coming in as the university-wide CRM, both maintained and funded centrally rather than something a standalone nursing marketing office would have to procure and pay for on its own. The program also borrows institutional credibility it couldn't manufacture alone: a 70-year history and a university-level brand that a brand-new, independent nursing marketing unit would have to build from scratch.

The caveat is that "better off inside" assumes those shared systems actually serve the program's specific recruitment timeline and audience, which is a question worth testing rather than assuming it's settled just because the systems exist.

The cost-of-entry test, with specifics

What has being inside the central structure actually cost the program. Porter's test isn't about the dollar cost of the shared systems themselves, it's about whether that cost has already eaten the benefit of being inside. Here's where that shows up concretely: the role that used to be split between a dedicated recruiter and a dedicated marketer is now one person covering both. That's the clearest cost of centralization, a structure that assumes shared, centralized systems will absorb some of the workload a two-person team used to split. If those systems aren't yet fully carrying that weight, mid-transition from MarketingCloud to Slate, the program is paying the staffing cost of centralization, one generalist instead of two specialists, without yet getting the full benefit the centralized systems were supposed to provide.

Make-or-buy on two specific functions: Google Ads and the CRM

Williamson's make-or-buy question, applied narrowly, gives two useful examples rather than one abstract point.

Google Ads as a "buy" decision. The School of Nursing pays for and runs its own Google Ads independently, it isn't a shared or centrally managed account. But the program still doesn't own the underlying platform: the account history, the algorithmic optimization Google's system has built up over time, and the keyword and audience data that drives performance all live inside Google's infrastructure, not the program's own. That's a textbook buy decision: efficient, because building an equivalent in-house ad-tech capability would be absurd for a single academic program, but it still carries a real switching cost if the program ever needed to restructure how campaigns are managed or move platforms. The lesson isn't to change anything about Google Ads. It's to recognize that a "buy" decision the program fully controls can still carry hold-up risk toward the vendor, just not toward the university.

The CRM function as a case for a defined, owned process layered on top of whichever system is active. Harrigan's framework suggests a middle option that fits here better than either full "make" (build an independent recruiting infrastructure the program doesn't have staff for) or full "buy" (hand the whole lead-management function to whichever central CRM is active and hope it works). That middle option is a standing, program-owned rule for the one handoff that actually determines whether a lead gets followed up on: how fast, and by what method, a lead collected at an event gets touched after the event ends. That rule can run on top of MarketingCloud today and move to Slate later without the program losing anything in the transition, because the rule is the thing the program should own, regardless of which system sits underneath it.

What we cut, and why

The full theory-of-the-firm foundation (Coase, Grossman & Hart, Hart & Moore, Jensen & Meckling) is cut this week because this chapter leads with the applied parenting and make-or-buy literature directly, and the formal property-rights framing would repeat ground already covered through appropriability in an earlier chapter. Diversification, economies of scope, the conglomerate discount, the AI value stack, and antitrust are all cut because they're university-level or market-level questions rather than program-level ones, and the AI stack question specifically is already settled at Boise State by the existence of the university's own AI tool.
