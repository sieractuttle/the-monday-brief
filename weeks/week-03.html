Week 03

Internal Analysis: Resources, Capabilities, VRIO

Two firms face the same five forces in the same industry and earn different returns. What do you have, or know how to do, that the firm next door can't copy?

The one idea

Most higher ed marketing teams can list what makes their program special: small class sizes, a signature lab, a beloved faculty member. VRIO is the test almost nobody runs on that list. Valuable, rare, costly to imitate, and is the organization actually set up to exploit it. Run it honestly and most programs find that half of what they lead with is table stakes, not an advantage. The idea worth keeping from this week's whole reading pile: an advantage isn't what you're proud of. It's what survives four questions, asked in order.

What would be wrong to leave out

Whether a data pile is actually a moat. It's tempting to treat "we have years of enrollment and engagement data" as an automatic advantage, especially with every AI conversation treating data as the new oil. Run it through VRIO instead of assuming it. Valuable, usually yes. Rare, only if competitors don't have comparable data, and most peer institutions have been collecting the same funnel metrics for just as long. Hard to imitate, this is where it gets interesting: data compounds, so a genuine head start is hard to close quickly. Organized to exploit, this is where most programs actually fail, sitting on years of data nobody is using to change a single recruitment decision. A pile of unused data isn't a moat. It's an unused advantage, and that's a workflow problem, not a bragging right.

Run it yourself

The VRIO practical test

Four questions, in order. Trace the tree, test one of your own program's talking points, then print the scorecard for the rest of your list.

How the test runs

Try it on your own program

Is it valuable?Can you point to it actually moving an outcome a prospective student cares about?
Is it rare?Check two or three competitor programs. Do they say the same thing?
Is it costly to imitate?Was it built slowly over years, or could a competitor stand it up this semester?
Are you organized to exploit it?Does it actually show up in recruitment content and admissions follow-up?

This week's field notes

Five things any higher ed marketer could do Monday

01 / 05Field note

Run VRIO on your top three talking points before you defend them to a dean.

Why

The test separates what's actually defensible from what just feels distinctive. — Barney

Do Monday

Pick your program's three most-repeated selling points and score each one: valuable, rare, hard to imitate, organized to exploit. Anything that fails at question one or two gets demoted from headline to footnote.

02 / 05Field note

Stop calling a shared resource a differentiator.

Why

A resource only counts as rare if your closest competitors genuinely lack it too. — Barney, 1995

Do Monday

Check two or three competitor program pages against your top claim. If they say the same thing, move it out of your unique value prop and treat it as table stakes.

03 / 05Field note

Weight slow-built assets over fast, visible ones.

Why

Advantages built up over years are harder to copy than a new campaign or redesign. — Dierickx & Cool

Do Monday

When prioritizing marketing investment, favor longitudinal assets, alumni outcomes, faculty reputation, employer relationships, over one-off visual refreshes, even when the refresh is more fun to work on.

04 / 05Field note

Find the unused advantage hiding inside your own team.

Why

A resource can be genuinely rare and hard to copy and still produce nothing, if nobody is organized to use it. — Barney, 1995

Do Monday

Pick your program's strongest VRIO-passing resource and trace it forward: does it show up in recruitment content? In admissions follow-up? Any missing step is a fixable gap, not a messaging problem.

05 / 05Field note

Run the data question honestly, not as a slogan.

Why

Data only becomes a moat if it compounds faster than competitors can catch up, and if someone is actually using it. — Hagiu & Wright

Do Monday

Name one decision your team made this month using historical enrollment or engagement data. If you can't name one, your "data advantage" is currently a pile, not a moat.
