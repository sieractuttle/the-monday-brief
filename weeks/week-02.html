Week 02

External Analysis: Five Forces to the Value Net

How to read the environment your program competes in, and what that picture tells you about where the growth actually comes from.

The one idea

Competitive analysis in higher ed marketing almost always stops at "who else offers this degree in our market." That's the Five Forces reflex, and it only tells you how to fight for a fixed pie. The real contribution of the Value Net is the complementor, a player who isn't competing with you but whose success expands demand for you. For nursing enrollment specifically, hospital systems desperate for BSN-prepared nurses, community college ADN programs feeding your RN-to-BS pipeline, state workforce funding tied to the nursing shortage. None of those are rivals. They're leverage to grow the whole market instead of just defending your slice of it. Most program marketing plans never name a single complementor. That's the gap.

What would be wrong to leave out

Supplier power. Five Forces treats it as a real constraint, and for nursing programs it usually is the actual ceiling on growth, not applicant demand. Clinical placement capacity is finite and shared across every nursing program in the region. You can generate all the leads you want, if there aren't enough clinical slots to place students, growth stops regardless of what marketing does. That's a different problem than a demand problem, and it needs a different owner.

This week's field notes

Five things any higher ed marketer could do Monday

01 / 05 Field note

Run the five forces on your specific program, not your whole college.

Why

Treating an entire college as one market hides where you actually have power. Different programs face different rivals, substitutes, and buyer power. — Porter

Do Monday

Pick one program you market and score buyer power, supplier power, substitutes, new entrants, and rivalry for that program alone, not the college as a whole. (Nursing example: score pre-licensure separately from RN-to-BS.)

02 / 05 Field note

Name your complementors, not just your competitors.

Why

The Value Net adds the one player most competitive scans skip, someone whose success increases the value of yours. — Brandenburger & Nalebuff

Do Monday

List three organizations whose success grows demand for your program: employers who hire your grads, feeder community colleges or high schools, professional associations, accrediting bodies with workforce mandates. Write down one thing each currently does for your recruitment, even informally.

03 / 05 Field note

Find your real bottleneck before you blame the funnel.

Why

Five Forces treats supplier power as a real constraint. For a lot of programs, the ceiling on growth isn't applicant demand, it's a capacity limit nobody in marketing controls. — Porter

Do Monday

Ask your program director what would stop you from enrolling twice as many students even with unlimited leads: faculty ratios, lab or studio space, advising capacity, clinical or internship placements, licensure exam seats. That number, not your lead volume, should set your recruitment targets.

04 / 05 Field note

Use PARTS to find a lever besides "advertise more."

Why

PARTS, players, added value, rules, tactics, scope, is a toolkit for changing the game, not just playing it harder. — Brandenburger & Nalebuff

Do Monday

Pick "players" and ask who could be added to the game, a new employer partner, articulation agreement, or community org, that would grow the whole applicant pool instead of just fighting over the current one.

05 / 05 Field note

Treat AI as a shift in the competitive landscape, not a new channel to add.

Why

This week's reading frames AI as something that reshapes competitive structure itself, not a marketing tool to bolt onto the existing stack. — Iansiti & Lakhani

Do Monday

Check whether a program you compete with is using AI for faster inquiry response, chat-based advising, or application follow-up. If so, name that as a shift in rivalry worth discussing with your team, not just a tactic to copy quietly.
