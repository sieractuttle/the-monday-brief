Week 06

Dynamic Capabilities and Appropriability: Why Having the Tool Isn't the Same as Capturing the Value

A program can sense an opportunity and even build toward the capability to act on it, and still lose the value to something as ordinary as a list of leads nobody got back to.

The ideas this chapter runs on

Dynamic capabilities are the ability to change, not the resources themselves. Teece's sense, seize, and reconfigure framework separates having good assets from being able to reorganize them when the environment shifts. A program can own a strong resource, a CRM, a reputation, a pipeline of leads, and still fail to capture value from it if the organization can't sense the opportunity in time, commit to seizing it, or reconfigure how work actually gets done around it.

Appropriability determines who actually captures the value of a capability, and it's often not the party that built it. Teece's 1986 argument is that innovators lose value constantly to whoever controls the complementary assets needed to act on it, distribution, service, or in this case, simply the staff time to follow up. That's not an abstract idea here. The program currently runs outreach through MarketingCloud and a set of manual processes, while a transition to Slate, Boise State's CRM, is underway to eventually automate lead follow-up. The tool is changing. The outcome so far has not: a list of leads collected at recruitment events over the past six months sat untouched, regardless of which system was technically in place. Appropriability was never really a tooling question. It's a question of whether the complementary asset, a person with the time and a defined process to act on a lead, exists to capture the value that lead represents. Neither MarketingCloud-plus-manual nor an unfinished Slate rollout supplies that on its own.

Architectural innovation changes how the parts connect, not the parts themselves. Henderson and Clark's distinction between component knowledge and architectural knowledge matters here because it's easy to mistake one for the other. Swapping MarketingCloud for Slate is a component change, one CRM for another. It is not, by itself, an architectural innovation. The actual architecture is how a lead moves from an event table to a CRM entry to a follow-up action, and who owns each handoff. Right now that architecture routes through one person for both recruitment and marketing. A new tool doesn't redesign that architecture. Only a deliberate decision about the handoffs does.

Timing and dominant design decide which format wins before quality ever gets debated. Suarez and Utterback's research on industry timing shows that early movers who commit to a workable design, even an imperfect one, often outlast later entrants with technically superior versions, because the market coalesces around the first credible standard. The lesson for a mid-transition CRM rollout is blunt: the cost of staying in a hybrid state, part MarketingCloud, part Slate, isn't just inefficiency. It's time spent not yet operating on any single dominant internal design, while competitors with a settled system keep moving.

AI raises the ceiling on execution speed well before it shows up in outcomes. The productivity J-curve research from Brynjolfsson, Rock, and Syverson found that general-purpose technologies produce a measurable lag between adoption and measured productivity gains, because the organization has to rebuild processes around the tool before the tool's value shows up in the numbers. That's a useful frame for a CRM transition too: the lag isn't a sign Slate is the wrong tool. It's the expected cost of rebuilding process around any new general-purpose system, and it's exactly the period where old leads are most likely to fall through.

What we cut, and why

This week's source set covers a wide range: dynamic capabilities, appropriability, architectural innovation, disruption theory, absorptive capacity, timing and dominant design, general-purpose technology, and network effects. Running one case study meant leaving out three threads that are strong on their own but would have pulled focus from the appropriability problem this chapter is built around.

Disruption theory, and King and Baatartogtokh's empirical audit of it. Christensen's framework and the corrective research on how loosely it actually holds up against real cases are worth knowing on their own terms. We cut it here because disruption theory is about a new entrant reshaping a market from below, and this week's problem is internal: an existing program's own process gap. Different question entirely.

Absorptive capacity and the exploration-exploitation tension. Cohen and Levinthal's argument about an organization's ability to recognize and use new information, and March's tension between exploring new approaches and exploiting known ones, both bear on a CRM transition. We cut them because this chapter already uses appropriability and architectural innovation to make a related point about capability versus capture, and adding a third capability-theory lens would have diluted rather than sharpened the case.

Network effects and format wars. Katz and Shapiro's work on competing standards is built for markets where user value depends on how many other people adopt the same format. A single program's internal CRM choice isn't that kind of contest. We kept the AI productivity J-curve instead, since it speaks directly to the transition currently underway.

The case study

The gap isn't the competitor. It's the six months of leads already sitting there.

What appropriability actually looks like inside Boise State's RN-BS recruitment process.

What actually happened. Over the past six months, recruitment events generated a list of interested leads, students who gave contact information at a table, an info session, or a community college visit. That list sat largely untouched. Not because the program lacks a CRM: MarketingCloud has been the system of record, run alongside manual processes to track and follow up with leads collected in person. Not because a better system isn't coming: a transition to Slate, the university's dedicated CRM, is underway specifically to automate this kind of follow-up. The tool wasn't the constraint. The capacity to act on what the tool captured was.

Why this matters more than any competitor comparison. This role was previously split into two positions: one person handled recruitment, a separate person handled marketing. It's now one role, covering both functions. That's not a staffing complaint, it's the actual organizational architecture this chapter is describing. A program like Grand Canyon University competes with dedicated recruitment and marketing teams built for volume and speed of follow-up. Boise State's RN-BS is, structurally, not resourced to match that pace lead-for-lead. Naming that plainly matters because it reframes the six-month gap: it isn't a performance problem to fix with more discipline or a faster CRM migration. It's a predictable outcome of the architecture, one person owning sensing, seizing, and reconfiguring the entire recruitment pipeline at once. Teece's framework says dynamic capability requires organizational capacity to act on what's sensed. Right now, that capacity is a single point of failure by design, not by accident.

What changes because of this. The fix isn't waiting for Slate to finish rolling out, and it isn't a message about the program's flexibility or affordability. It's building a standing rule, independent of which CRM is active, that any lead collected at a recruitment event gets one defined follow-up touch within a set window, even if that touch is a short, templated message rather than a personalized one. That rule can run on MarketingCloud today and move to Slate later without losing anything, because it fixes the architecture, the handoff from event to action, rather than waiting on a tool swap to fix a process gap the tool alone can't close.
