Week 04

Business- and Functional-Level Strategy: Operational Effectiveness, Blue Ocean, and the AI Floor

When every firm can copy your operational moves, what is strategy actually for? And what changes when AI raises everyone's operational floor at once?

The three ideas this chapter runs on

Operational effectiveness is not strategy. Porter's 1996 argument, still the anchor of this whole field decades later: doing the same things as competitors, just faster or better, raises the floor for everyone and gets competed away within a season. Strategy is choosing a different, narrower set of activities and accepting the trade-offs that come with it. The two get confused constantly because operational effectiveness feels like progress, better email open rates, faster response times, while contributing nothing to an actual position.

Blue Ocean asks what you'd cut, not just what you'd add. Kim and Mauborgne's value innovation work, and the eliminate-reduce-raise-create grid specifically, is a tool for getting out of head-to-head competition. Instead of matching competitors feature for feature, it forces a real choice: what can you eliminate that the category assumes is necessary, what can you reduce, what should you raise well past the category norm, and what should you create that doesn't exist yet.

AI raises the floor, which is exactly why it's not a strategy. The Jagged Technological Frontier study found a genuine productivity boost from AI, but an uneven one. Once every competing program has access to the same AI tools for drafting emails or generating social copy, that capability stops being a differentiator the moment everyone has it. What's left as the actual edge is judgment: knowing where the AI's output can be trusted and where it can't, and knowing when and to whom something should be sent, not how fast it got written.

What we cut, and why

This week's source set is built around five sub-themes: generic strategy and positioning, the trade-offs argument, Blue Ocean, Deming and systems thinking, and AI at the business level. We're running one continuous case instead of five separate pieces, which meant cutting two threads that are genuinely worth reading on their own terms but would have split the chapter's focus.

Deming and the Red Bead experiment. The systems-thinking argument, that most performance variation comes from the system and not the individual, is one of the sharpest ideas in this week's whole reading list. We cut it here because it's a different diagnostic question (why did this outcome happen) than the one this chapter is answering (what should the strategy be), not because it's less important.

The 4Ps of AI Partnership. A genuinely useful daily framework for deciding whether a task is execution or exploration and who's setting the frame. We cut it because this chapter already uses the AI-floor argument to make a related point, and running both frameworks in one piece would have doubled the AI discussion without adding a new idea.

The case study

Same tools, same floor

What Boise State's RN-BS actually has that GCU and WGU don't.

The setup. Three RN-to-BSN programs, same customer, same basic pitch: come finish your BSN online, keep working. Grand Canyon University runs a fixed 14-month, one-course-at-a-time format at $340 per credit. Western Governors University runs the largest RN-to-BSN program in the country on a competency-based flat rate, finish faster, pay less, roughly $5,325 per six-month term. Boise State's RN-BS offers flat-rate tuition regardless of state residency, NC-SARA authorization in all 50 states, and an 18-month completion timeline.

Act 1: the operational effectiveness trap. Look at what actually gets said in outreach across all three programs, flexible, affordable, finish your BSN around your work schedule, flat-rate tuition. That's the problem. "Flat-rate tuition" is not a Boise State differentiator when WGU has built its entire national brand on flat-rate, finish-faster pricing for over 150,000 students. Leading with it means competing on the one axis where the biggest player in the category already owns the perception. That's operational effectiveness: doing a real, good thing, just not a thing that's yours.

Act 2: the pivot. Run it through the eliminate-reduce-raise-create grid.

Eliminate · Reduce · Raise · Create — Boise State RN-BS

Eliminate The gap between finishing an ADN and starting BSN coursework. Neither GCU's fixed terms nor WGU's competency model let a student start before their associate degree is done. Boise State's dual enrollment pathway does.
Reduce Flat-rate tuition as the lead message. It's true, but it's WGU's category, not Boise State's, and leading with it invites a comparison Boise State can't win on scale.
Raise Named, direct access to program staff instead of the "largest program in the country" facelessness that comes with 150,000 WGU students.
Create Messaging built specifically for the moment a student is still finishing their ADN, delivered at the exact events where those students already are, community college welcome nights, ADN program tabling, not a generic "come back later" message.

The position that falls out isn't "we're also flexible and affordable." It's "you don't have to wait to start." Nobody else in this comparison can say that truthfully.

Act 3: what changes about AI once you have this. GCU and WGU can both generate the same kind of AI-drafted "why get your BSN" blog post, the same chatbot-assisted admissions FAQ, at the same quality, this week. That capability is now the floor, not an edge. The strategic move isn't writing that content faster. It's using AI to help spot the exact moment in the ADN pipeline, right after a specific community college's welcome night, right when transcript review would already flag someone as eligible, when the "start now" message should go out, and to whom. The judgment about timing and audience is the part a competitor with the same AI subscription can't copy. The drafted email is not.
